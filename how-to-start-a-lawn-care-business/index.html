How to Start a Lawn Care Business: Equipment, Pricing and First Customers

Updated November 12, 2026 · 3 min read

Lawn care is one of the most accessible businesses you can start. Teenagers do it with the family mower; career landscapers build it into crews and trucks. The difference between a summer side hustle and a real business is a handful of decisions you make at the start. Here they are, in order.

1. Choose your services

Start with a narrow menu you can deliver well:

  • Mowing, trimming and blowing. The core weekly service.
  • Spring and fall cleanups. Leaves, debris, bed edging. High value, once or twice a year.
  • Hedge and shrub trimming.
  • Mulching and bed maintenance.
  • Aeration and overseeding.

Fertilizer and weed control can be profitable, but many states require a pesticide applicator license for some products. Check before you offer it.

2. Handle licenses and insurance

  • Check your city or county for a general business license.
  • Register your business name if it's not your own name, and get a free EIN.
  • Open a separate bank account.
  • Buy general liability insurance. A rock thrown from a mower into a car window or a sliding glass door is a real risk.
  • If you hire help, ask about workers' compensation requirements in your state.

3. Buy equipment in stages

You don't need a zero-turn mower on day one. A sensible progression:

Stage Equipment Notes
Starter Quality push or self-propelled mower, string trimmer, blower, gas cans, safety glasses and ear protection Enough for small residential yards
Growing Commercial walk-behind or stand-on mower, edger, open trailer Faster on medium and large lots
Established Zero-turn mower, backup trimmer and blower, enclosed trailer When your route is full

Buy commercial-grade trimmers and blowers early; they last much longer. Track every purchase for taxes.

4. Price for profit

Work out your target hourly rate, time a few typical yards, and set per-cut prices by lot size, adjusting for slopes, obstacles and access. Our guide on how much to charge for lawn mowing gives example rates and a simple formula.

5. Build a tight route

Drive time is the silent profit killer. Ten yards on the same street earn far more than ten yards spread across town. When you start marketing:

  • Pick one or two neighborhoods and saturate them.
  • Offer a small discount to neighbors of existing customers.
  • Group customers by day so you mow one area each day.

6. Get your paperwork ready

Professional paperwork makes a one-person crew look established and keeps everyone clear on what is included:

  • Client and property form for gate codes, pets, problem areas and special instructions
  • Job estimate with services, frequency and price per visit
  • Seasonal service plan showing what you'll do each month
  • Crew job checklist so every visit is consistent
  • Service agreement with rain delay, skip, payment and cancellation policies (here's what to include)
  • Invoice

7. Find your first customers

  • Door hangers and flyers in your target neighborhood, ideally right after you mow a yard there.
  • Nextdoor and local Facebook groups. Post photos of crisp stripes and clean edges.
  • Google Business Profile. Free and essential for "lawn care near me."
  • Ask every customer for a referral and offer a free cut for each new customer they send.
  • Realtors and property managers need vacant properties mowed and listings cleaned up.

8. Plan for the off-season

In colder climates mowing stops for months. Plan for it:

  • Sell seasonal contracts billed monthly all year.
  • Add fall cleanups, gutter cleaning, holiday light installation or snow removal.
  • Use winter to service equipment and line up next season's customers.

9. Track your money

Log every cut, payment and expense. Know your profit per yard; some yards look profitable until you add the drive time. Set aside money for taxes from every payment.
