Pet Sitting Contract: What to Include (Plus the Forms You Need)

Updated November 12, 2026 · 3 min read

When you care for someone's pet in their home, you take on real responsibility. A clear contract and a few simple forms protect the pet, the owner and you. They also show new clients you are a professional, which is often what wins the booking over a cheaper sitter.

This is general information, not legal advice. Laws vary by state, so consider having a local professional review your final contract.

The forms every pet sitter should collect

A contract covers the business terms. These forms cover the pet and the home:

Form What it captures
New client form Owner contact details, address, emergency contacts, home access, alarm codes, Wi-Fi, house rules
Pet profile Name, breed, age, feeding schedule, medications, behavior quirks, fears, leash and potty habits
Vet and emergency release Vet clinic details and the owner's permission and spending limit for emergency treatment
Visit report card What happened at each visit: food, water, potty, meds, mood, notes

The vet release is the one sitters forget, and it's the one that matters most in an emergency. Without it, a clinic may wait to reach the owner before treating.

What to include in the contract

1. Parties and services

Your business details, the client's details, the pets covered, and the services booked: drop-ins, walks, overnights, and dates and times.

2. Rates and payment

Price per visit or per night, extra pet fees, holiday rates, when payment is due (many sitters require payment before the booking starts) and accepted methods.

3. Cancellation policy

How much notice the client must give for a refund, and any stricter rules for holidays. Also what happens if you need to cancel, such as illness, and whether you have a backup sitter.

4. Home access and keys

How you receive and return keys or codes, and what parts of the home you may use. For overnight stays, note house rules: guests, using the TV, kitchen use.

5. Health and vaccinations

The owner confirms the pet is vaccinated as required, is in good health, and has disclosed any aggression, bite history or medical conditions.

6. Emergencies and vet care

Permission for you to take the pet to the vet in an emergency, who pays, and the spending limit if the owner can't be reached. Reference the separate vet release form.

7. Liability

What you are and are not responsible for. For example, injuries or illness that happen despite reasonable care, damage caused by the pet, or problems from information the owner didn't disclose. Mention your pet sitter insurance if you carry it.

8. Late return and extended stays

The fee if the owner returns later than planned and how extra days are billed.

9. Photos and social media

Whether you can post photos of the pet online. Many clients love it; some don't.

10. Signatures and date

Both parties sign. Keep a copy of every contract and form with the client's records.

When to get the paperwork signed

Do it at the meet-and-greet, a free visit before the first booking where you meet the pet, walk through the home and review the forms together. It's also the perfect time to spot anything the owner forgot to mention.

Keep records up to date

Ask returning clients to update the pet profile and vet release once a year or whenever medications change. A visit report card after every booking keeps clients happy and gives you a record if questions come up later.
