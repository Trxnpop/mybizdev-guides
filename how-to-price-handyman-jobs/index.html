How to Price Handyman Jobs: Hourly, Flat Rate and Minimum Charges

Updated November 12, 2026 · 3 min read

Handyman work is wide open: one day you hang shelves, the next you replace a faucet and patch drywall. That variety makes pricing tricky. Charge too little and you work hard for nothing; charge too much without explaining it and clients go elsewhere. This guide gives you a simple pricing structure that works for almost any small job.

Start with your real hourly cost

Your hourly rate has to pay for much more than your time on the job. Add up:

  • Your pay. What you want to earn per hour.
  • Taxes. Self-employed people in the US pay roughly 15% self-employment tax on top of income tax.
  • Vehicle. Fuel, maintenance and insurance on the truck or van.
  • Tools. Replacing and repairing tools, blades, bits and consumables.
  • Insurance and licensing. Liability insurance, plus any license your state requires.
  • Unpaid time. Driving, quoting, buying materials and invoicing.

Many experienced handymen find their break-even cost is $30 to $45 an hour before they earn anything. That is why rates in much of the US commonly fall in the $60 to $100 an hour range, and higher in expensive cities. Look up a few local competitors to see where your market sits.

Set a minimum charge

Small jobs are the trap. Driving 25 minutes to tighten a cabinet hinge can cost you more than you earn. A minimum charge fixes that.

  • A common approach is a one or two hour minimum, or a flat "service call" fee that includes the first hour.
  • Encourage clients to make a "honey-do list" so you can stack several small tasks into one visit.

Clients accept minimums when you explain them up front. Put yours on your rate sheet and in your agreement.

Hourly vs flat rate: when to use each

Use hourly when Use a flat price when
You can't see the full problem (leaks, old wiring, rot) The job is repeatable (mount a TV, install a faucet)
The client keeps adding tasks You know how long it takes you
It is a repair with unknowns The client wants a firm number before you start

Flat pricing often earns you more as you get faster, because the client pays for the result, not your time. Build each flat price from your hourly rate: estimated hours × rate + materials + a small buffer for surprises.

Example flat prices (starting points only)

Prices vary widely by region. These are illustrations of how to structure a price, not a quote.

  • Mount a TV on drywall (client supplies mount): about 1 to 1.5 hours of labor
  • Replace a standard faucet: about 1 to 2 hours plus parts
  • Patch a fist-sized drywall hole: 1 hour of labor spread across two visits for drying, or charge for a return trip
  • Assemble flat-pack furniture: price by piece or by the hour

Mark up materials

When you buy materials, you spend time picking them up and you take on the risk if they are wrong or returned. Many handymen add 10% to 25% on top of the receipt, or charge a trip fee for supply runs. Be transparent: list materials on the estimate and invoice so clients can see what they paid for.

Charge for the extras

Add separate line items for:

  • Disposal. Hauling away old fixtures, drywall scraps or a broken appliance.
  • After-hours or emergency calls. Evenings, weekends and same-day calls commonly carry 1.5x the normal rate.
  • Travel outside your area. A trip fee for long drives.
  • Return trips. If the client needs a second visit because parts were not ready.

Put every price in writing

A written estimate protects you from "I thought that included..." conversations. It should list each task, the labor, materials, any fees and what is not included. If the job changes once you start, write up a change and get approval before doing the extra work. We cover what to include in how to write a handyman estimate.

Know what you can legally do

Many states limit the size of jobs a handyman can take on without a contractor's license, and electrical, plumbing and gas work often require a licensed trade. Check your state's rules before you quote larger jobs.
