How Much to Charge for Lawn Mowing (and How to Quote Any Yard)

Updated November 12, 2026 · 3 min read

Lawn mowing looks simple to price until you are standing in front of a yard with a slope, three flower beds and a trampoline. Charge per cut without a system and you will overcharge some customers and lose money on others. Here is how to price mowing so every yard pays you fairly.

The three ways to price mowing

Method How it works Best for
Per cut (flat rate) One price per visit for that yard Recurring residential customers
By square footage A rate per 1,000 sq ft of turf Quoting new yards consistently
Hourly Charge per hour on site Overgrown, one-time or unusual jobs

Most lawn care businesses quote per cut, but they figure out that flat price using square footage and how long the yard takes.

Know your per-hour target

Start with what an hour of mowing needs to earn. Include your pay, fuel, equipment wear and repairs, insurance, trailer or truck costs, and the time spent driving between yards. Many solo operators aim for something like $50 to $75 per hour of mowing time once equipment is paid for, but your market decides the ceiling.

Then time yourself. If a typical quarter-acre lot takes you 40 minutes including trimming and blowing, and you want $60 an hour, that yard needs to be about $40 per cut.

Example per-cut pricing by lot size

These are illustrations of how to structure prices. Real rates swing widely by region, so call two or three competitors before you set yours.

Lot size Typical time (mow, trim, blow) Example price per cut
Small (under 5,000 sq ft) 20 to 30 min $30 to $45
Medium (5,000 to 10,000 sq ft) 30 to 45 min $40 to $60
Quarter acre (about 11,000 sq ft) 40 to 55 min $45 to $70
Half acre 60 to 90 min $65 to $100
Acre and up Varies Quote by the hour or per acre

Adjust for what slows you down

Two yards with the same square footage can take very different amounts of time. Add to your base price for:

  • Slopes and hills that need a push mower or extra care
  • Obstacles like trees, beds, play sets and lots of edging
  • Gates and narrow access that mean you can't bring the bigger mower
  • Overgrowth on the first cut (often 1.5x to 2x the normal price)
  • Clippings bagged and hauled instead of mulched
  • Distance from the rest of your route

Add-on services that raise your average ticket

Mowing gets you in the door. Add-ons are where profit grows:

  • Edging and trimming (often included, sometimes extra)
  • Leaf cleanup in fall
  • Hedge and shrub trimming
  • Mulching and bed weeding
  • Aeration and overseeding
  • Spring and fall cleanups
  • Fertilizing (many states require a license for applying pesticides or some fertilizers, so check first)

Weekly, biweekly and seasonal pricing

Weekly customers keep your route tight and the grass shorter, which makes each cut faster. Many businesses charge biweekly customers a bit more per cut because the grass is longer.

Seasonal contracts help both sides. You quote a full season (for example, 28 weekly cuts plus a spring and fall cleanup) and bill in equal monthly payments. The customer gets a predictable bill and you get steady cash flow, even in the slow weeks.

Write it down on an estimate

Give every new customer a written estimate that lists the property, the services, frequency, price per visit and what happens with rain delays and skipped weeks. It prevents arguments later and makes you look established.
