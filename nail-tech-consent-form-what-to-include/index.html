Nail Tech Consent Form and Client Intake: What to Include

Updated November 13, 2026 · 3 min read

A signed intake and consent form is one of the simplest ways for a nail tech to protect clients and themselves. It captures health information that affects how you work, records that the client understood the service, and gives you a paper trail if a client has a reaction or infection later.

This is general information, not legal or medical advice. Licensing and sanitation rules vary by state, so check with your board and your insurer.

Health questions to ask

Ask every new client, and ask returning clients to update their answers regularly:

  • Allergies, especially to acrylates, gel products, latex or fragrances
  • Previous reactions to nail products (itching, redness, swelling around the nail)
  • Nail or skin conditions: fungal infections, psoriasis, eczema, warts
  • Diabetes or circulation problems, which matter especially for pedicures
  • Blood thinners or conditions that affect healing
  • Pregnancy, if relevant to your products or policies
  • Recent injuries or cuts on the hands or feet
  • Sensitivity to fumes or dust

If an answer concerns you, it's appropriate to adjust the service, delay it or suggest the client checks with a doctor.

What the consent and release should include

  1. The service: manicure, pedicure, gel, acrylic, dip, extensions, nail art or removal.
  2. Possible risks: allergic reactions to acrylates and gels, irritation, thinning of the natural nail, lifting, and infection if aftercare is not followed.
  3. Client responsibilities: accurate health information, following aftercare, not picking or peeling product, and booking proper removal.
  4. What to do if there's a problem: contact you, and seek medical advice for signs of a reaction or infection.
  5. Results: wear time varies with nail health, lifestyle and aftercare.
  6. Photo release: optional permission to post photos of their nails.
  7. Signatures and date.

Keep a service record for every client

Record each appointment: service, products and colors used, nail shape and length, any issues you noticed and recommendations. It helps you recreate a favorite set, spot patterns (like a client who always lifts on one finger) and respond professionally if questions come up.

Pair the form with studio policies

Most client disputes are about lateness, cancellations, fixes and refunds. Put your deposit, lateness, cancellation and repair policies in writing and have clients acknowledge them at booking. A clear fix window (for example, free repairs within a few days if aftercare was followed) prevents arguments.

Give aftercare in writing

A printed aftercare card covering cuticle oil, gloves for cleaning, not using nails as tools and booking professional removal reduces breakage and reaction risk, and makes your studio look polished.
