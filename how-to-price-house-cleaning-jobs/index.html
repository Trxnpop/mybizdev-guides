How to Price House Cleaning Jobs (Without Undercharging)

Updated November 12, 2026 · 4 min read

Pricing is where most new cleaners lose money. They pick a number that sounds friendly, book a few homes, and three months later realize they are working for less than minimum wage once gas and supplies come out. This guide gives you a repeatable way to price any home so every quote covers your time, your costs and a profit.

Step 1: Work out the hourly rate you need

Before you think about bedrooms or square feet, decide what an hour of your time has to earn. Start with what you want to take home, then add the costs that come out of every job.

  1. Target pay. Say you want $25 an hour in your pocket.
  2. Self-employment tax. In the US, add roughly 15% for Social Security and Medicare if you are self-employed.
  3. Supplies and gas. Many solo cleaners spend $5 to $10 per job on products, cloths, vacuum bags and fuel.
  4. Unpaid time. Driving, quoting, texting clients and bookkeeping are real work. If you spend 1 hour of unpaid time for every 4 hours cleaning, your billable hours need to cover 5 hours of effort.

Put together, a cleaner who wants $25 an hour take-home usually needs to bill somewhere around $35 to $45 an hour. That is why "I'll charge $20 an hour" almost never works.

Quick check: (target hourly pay × 1.15 tax) ÷ 0.8 billable share + supply cost per hour = minimum hourly rate.

Step 2: Choose a pricing method

There are four common ways to quote. You will probably use a flat price with the client and an hourly rate behind the scenes.

Method How it works Good for Watch out for
Hourly Bill each hour, per cleaner Clutter, first cleans, unusual homes Clients worry the clock will run long
Per room A set price per bedroom and bathroom Easy to explain over the phone Big rooms and small rooms pay the same
Square foot A rate per square foot Large or empty homes, move-outs Ignores bathrooms, which take the longest
Flat rate (blended) Base price plus beds, baths and square feet Recurring clients Needs a walkthrough or good photos

Most clients prefer a flat price. They want to know what they will pay before you arrive. The trick is to build that flat price from your hourly rate, so you always know what you are earning.

Step 3: Build a flat price from beds, baths and square feet

Here is a simple formula many cleaners use:

Base fee + (bedrooms × bedroom rate) + (bathrooms × bathroom rate) + square foot adjustment

Example starting rates for a standard clean (adjust for your area):

  • Base fee: $60 (covers kitchen, living areas, travel and setup)
  • Each bedroom: $15
  • Each full bathroom: $25 (half bath $12)
  • Homes over 2,000 sq ft: add $10 to $15 per extra 500 sq ft

A 3-bed, 2-bath, 1,800 sq ft home comes to $60 + $45 + $50 = $155 for a standard clean. If you know that house takes you about 3.5 hours, that is roughly $44 an hour. Right where you want to be.

Step 4: Price deep cleans and move-outs differently

A first-time or deep clean takes much longer than a maintenance visit. Do not price it the same.

  • Deep clean: 1.5x to 2x your standard price. It covers baseboards, inside the microwave, door frames, light switches and built-up grime.
  • Move-in or move-out: 2x to 2.5x standard. Empty homes are easier to move around in, but inside cabinets, inside the oven and the fridge add hours.
  • Add-ons: price separately. Common ones are inside the fridge ($25 to $40), inside the oven ($25 to $40), interior windows ($5 to $8 per window), laundry ($15 to $25 per load) and changing linens.

Many cleaners require a deep clean before starting recurring service. It resets the home so your maintenance price stays fair.

Step 5: Offer recurring discounts that still make you money

Recurring clients are the backbone of a cleaning business because the home stays cleaner between visits. A common discount structure looks like this:

Frequency Discount off standard price
Weekly 15% to 20%
Every two weeks 10% to 15%
Monthly 0% to 10%
One time No discount

Only discount the visits that actually get faster. If a monthly client's home looks the same as a first clean every time, charge closer to your standard rate.

Step 6: Check your quote against real time

Every price is a guess until you clean the home. After the first two or three visits, compare the time you spent with what you charged. If you are earning less than your minimum hourly rate, raise the price or trim the task list. Write it down every time so you are working from real numbers, not memory.

Common pricing mistakes

  • Matching the cheapest cleaner on Facebook. Someone will always be cheaper. Compete on reliability and communication instead.
  • Quoting over text without details. Ask for beds, baths, square feet, pets and the date of the last professional clean.
  • Forgetting travel. Add a trip fee for homes outside your normal area.
  • Never raising prices. Plan a small increase once a year and give clients 30 days' notice.
