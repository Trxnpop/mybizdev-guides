How to Price Nail Services as a Nail Tech

Updated November 13, 2026 · 3 min read

Nail techs often set prices by copying the salon down the street. But your costs, your speed and your skill level are your own. Pricing from your real numbers means every appointment pays you properly, and you'll know exactly when it's time to raise prices.

Step 1: Work out your hourly target

Add up monthly costs: suite or booth rent, products, tools, disposables, sanitation supplies, insurance, license renewals, booking software, card fees and education. Divide by the hours you are actually booked. Add your desired hourly pay plus roughly 15% for self-employment tax (US). That's the minimum each service hour must earn.

Step 2: Time your services honestly

Newer techs are slower, and that's fine. Price from your real times now, and raise prices as you get faster and better.

Step 3: Build your service menu

Example structure (rates vary widely by city and experience, so check five or six techs near you):

Service Typical time How to price
Classic manicure 30 to 45 min Flat price
Gel manicure 45 to 60 min Flat price
Acrylic or hard gel full set 75 to 120 min Base price by length
Fill (2 to 3 weeks) 60 to 90 min Lower than a full set, with a time limit
Dip powder 60 to 75 min Flat price
Pedicure 45 to 75 min Tiered (basic, deluxe)
Removal 15 to 30 min Flat fee, or included with a new set

Step 4: Charge for length and art

Longer nails take more product and time. Many techs price sets by length tier (short, medium, long, extra long). Price nail art separately:

  • Simple art (a few accent nails, dots, chrome): small per-nail or flat fee
  • Medium art (French tips, ombré, stickers on all nails)
  • Complex art (hand-painted designs, 3D, charms): priced by time

Show photos of each art level on your menu so clients know what they're getting.

Step 5: Price repairs and fixes

  • Nail repair or replacement: per nail
  • Free fix window: for example, repairs within a few days if aftercare was followed
  • Foreign fills (another tech's work): extra time to correct, so price it higher or require a new set

Step 6: Protect your schedule

Deposits for long appointments and new clients, plus late and cancellation fees, keep your book full. Put the policy in writing and have clients agree when they book.

Step 7: Reward regulars

A small discount for clients who pre-book their next fill keeps your calendar predictable. Avoid deep discounts for new clients; they attract bargain hunters who rarely rebook.

When to raise prices

Raise prices when you're booked out more than two weeks, after advanced training, or once a year. Give regulars notice, and consider a short grace period at the old price.
