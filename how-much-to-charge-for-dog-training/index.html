How Much to Charge for Dog Training (Private, Group and Board and Train)

Updated November 19, 2026 · 3 min read

Dog training is a business built on results, and clients will pay well for a trainer they trust. New trainers usually make the same mistake: they price single lessons cheaply and hope clients book more. A better approach is to price programs that are long enough to actually change behavior. Here is how to build a price list.

The main ways trainers charge

  • Private lessons: one-on-one sessions at the client's home, a park or your facility. Usually 45 to 60 minutes.
  • Packages: a set number of private lessons sold together, such as 4 or 6 sessions. This is where most trainers make their money.
  • Group classes: a 4 to 6 week course for several dogs at once, such as puppy manners or basic obedience.
  • Board and train: the dog stays with you for one to four weeks of daily training, followed by handoff lessons with the owner.
  • Day training: you pick up the dog or visit during the day and train while the owner is at work.

Example starting rates

Rates vary widely by city and by the trainer's experience. Check local trainers before you settle on your own prices. Common US starting points:

Service Typical starting price
Single private lesson (in home) $75 to $150
4-lesson private package $275 to $550
6-lesson private package $400 to $800
Group class, 6 weeks $150 to $300 per dog
Puppy class, 4 to 6 weeks $120 to $250 per dog
Behavior consultation (reactivity, aggression) $125 to $250 for the first session
Board and train, 2 weeks $1,200 to $2,500
Travel fee beyond your service area $10 to $40
Extra dog in the same household add 25% to 50%

Why packages beat single lessons

A dog doesn't learn loose-leash walking in one hour. Clients who buy one lesson often get partial results and decide training "didn't work." A 4 or 6 session package lets you set goals, assign homework and actually deliver results, which leads to referrals and good reviews.

Price the package so it's a small discount compared to buying lessons one at a time, and require payment up front or in two installments.

Start every client with an assessment

A short behavior assessment at the first session tells you what the dog needs and helps you recommend the right program. Record the dog's history, triggers, health issues and the owner's goals. It protects you too: if a dog has a bite history, you need to know before you start.

Put it in a training agreement

Every client should sign a training agreement and waiver that covers:

  • What the program includes and how many sessions
  • Payment, cancellation and rescheduling policies
  • That results depend on the owner practicing between sessions
  • Vaccination requirements, especially for group classes
  • A liability waiver for injuries or damage during training

After each session, send short session notes and homework. Clients who practice get better results, and clients who see progress buy the next package.
