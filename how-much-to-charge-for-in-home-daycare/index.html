How Much to Charge for In-Home Daycare (Weekly Rates and Policies)

Updated November 19, 2026 · 3 min read

In-home daycare is one of the few businesses where you can earn a full-time income without leaving your house. Parents also expect clear, consistent rates and written policies, so it pays to set them carefully from day one. Here is how to work out weekly tuition, what to charge extra for, and what belongs in your parent agreement.

Start with your local market

Childcare prices depend heavily on where you live. Before you set rates, find out what other home daycares and centers near you charge:

  • Call or check websites of two or three centers nearby
  • Ask in local parent groups what families pay for home daycare
  • Check your state's childcare resource and referral agency, which often publishes average local rates

Most home daycares price a little below local centers while offering smaller groups and more flexibility.

Charge by the week, not the day

Nearly all providers charge a flat weekly rate whether or not the child attends every day. Your costs (your time, food, supplies, your home) don't go away when a child stays home, and the spot is held for them. Bill weekly or every two weeks, paid in advance.

Example rate structure

These ranges are common in many US areas, but your local rates may be quite different. Use the structure, then plug in your own numbers.

Care type Typical weekly rate
Infant (under 12 to 18 months), full time $200 to $400
Toddler, full time $175 to $325
Preschool age, full time $150 to $300
Part time (2 to 3 days a week) 60% to 75% of the full-time rate
School-age, before and after school $75 to $175
Drop-in, per day (if you offer it) $50 to $90

Infants cost more because they need more one-on-one attention and count more heavily toward most state ratio limits.

Fees that should be in writing

  • Registration fee: a one-time fee (often $50 to $150) to hold a spot and cover paperwork
  • Late pickup fee: for example $1 to $2 per minute after closing time
  • Late payment fee: a flat fee if tuition is not paid by the due date
  • Extended hours: an hourly rate for care outside your normal hours
  • Meals: included, or a weekly add-on if parents send food

Vacation, sick days and holidays

This is where most disputes happen, so decide now:

  • Your vacation: many providers take one to two paid weeks a year and list paid holidays in the contract.
  • Family vacation: most charge full or half tuition to hold the spot while the family is away.
  • Sick days: tuition stays the same. Spell out your illness policy (fever, vomiting, contagious conditions) and when a child can return.

What goes in a parent agreement

Before a child starts, both sides should sign a parent agreement that covers:

  • Hours, weekly tuition, due date and accepted payment methods
  • All fees listed above
  • Vacation, holiday and sick-day policies
  • Who is authorized to pick up the child
  • Notice required to leave care (two weeks is common)
  • Emergency contacts and medical consent

Also check your state's licensing rules. Most states require a license once you care for more than a set number of unrelated children, and licensing affects how many children you can enroll.
