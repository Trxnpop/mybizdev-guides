How to Start a House Cleaning Business: A Step-by-Step Checklist

Updated November 12, 2026 · 4 min read

House cleaning is one of the cheapest service businesses to start. You can begin with a few hundred dollars of supplies, no storefront and no special degree. What separates the cleaners who are fully booked from the ones who quit after a month is mostly setup: clear prices, simple paperwork and a plan for finding clients. Here is the checklist.

1. Decide what kind of cleaning you will offer

Pick a lane before you print anything. Your choice affects your prices, supplies and marketing.

  • Recurring residential cleaning. Weekly or biweekly visits to the same homes. Steady, predictable income.
  • Deep cleans and move-outs. Higher price per job, but you are always looking for the next client.
  • Short-term rental turnovers. Fast cleans between Airbnb or vacation rental guests. Tight schedules, but hosts book often.
  • Small offices. Usually evenings, often with a monthly contract.

Most new cleaners start with recurring residential and add deep cleans as a profitable extra.

2. Handle the legal basics

Requirements vary by state, county and city, so check locally. In most of the US the basics are:

  • Business name. If you use a name other than your own, you may need to register a "doing business as" (DBA) with your county or state.
  • Business license. Many cities and counties require a general business license. Call your city clerk or search your city's website.
  • Structure. Many solo cleaners start as a sole proprietor. Some form an LLC for liability separation. A local accountant can tell you which makes sense.
  • Tax ID. You can get a free EIN from the IRS website. It lets you avoid giving clients your Social Security number.
  • Separate bank account. Keep business money separate from day one. It makes taxes much easier.

3. Get insured (and consider bonding)

General liability insurance covers you if you break something or someone gets hurt. Many clients will ask whether you are insured, and saying yes helps you win jobs. A janitorial bond, which protects clients against theft by an employee, is common once you hire help. Get quotes from a few providers and ask specifically about cleaning businesses.

4. Buy your starter supplies

You do not need a van full of equipment. A practical starter kit:

  • A good vacuum with attachments (your biggest purchase)
  • Microfiber cloths, color-coded for kitchen, bathroom and dusting
  • Mop and bucket or a flat microfiber mop
  • All-purpose cleaner, glass cleaner, bathroom cleaner, degreaser and a disinfectant
  • Scrub brushes, a grout brush, sponges and a pumice stone for tough rings
  • A caddy to carry everything room to room
  • Gloves, trash bags and a step stool

Ask clients whether they want you to use your products or theirs, and note any allergies on their intake form.

5. Set your prices before you talk to anyone

Nothing kills confidence like making up a price on the phone. Work out your minimum hourly rate, then build flat prices from bedrooms, bathrooms and square feet. Our full guide on how to price house cleaning jobs walks through the math with examples.

Write your prices down as a simple service menu: standard clean, deep clean, move-out, and add-ons like inside the oven or fridge.

6. Get your paperwork in order

Professional paperwork is how a one-person business looks established. At minimum you want:

Form Why you need it
Client intake form Captures address, entry instructions, pets, alarm codes, allergies and priorities
Walkthrough and estimate Lets you quote accurately and gets the client to agree in writing
Room-by-room checklist Keeps every clean consistent, and helps when you hire help
Service agreement Sets cancellation, lockout, payment and breakage policies
Invoice Gets you paid on time and gives you a record for taxes
Feedback card Turns happy clients into reviews and referrals

7. Set up a simple system for money

Track every job, payment and expense from the start. You need to know which clients have not paid, what you spent on supplies and how much profit you made each month. A spreadsheet is enough for your first year. Save receipts for supplies, mileage and insurance, since they may reduce your taxes.

Decide how you will take payment: cash, check, a payment app or card. Put your payment terms (for example, due on the day of service) in your agreement.

8. Find your first five clients

Your first clients usually come from people who already know you. Then the business spreads by word of mouth.

  1. Tell everyone. Friends, family, coworkers, your gym, your kid's school. Ask for introductions, not favors.
  2. Local Facebook groups and Nextdoor. Post a friendly introduction with a photo of you, your service area and a simple offer.
  3. Google Business Profile. It is free and puts you on Google Maps when people search "house cleaner near me."
  4. Realtors and property managers. They constantly need move-out and listing-prep cleans.
  5. Ask for reviews after every great clean. Hand over a feedback card or text a review link the same day.

9. Deliver a consistent clean every time

Clients keep a cleaner because they never have to think about it. Use the same checklist every visit, show up when you said you would and communicate early if anything changes. Leave a short note after the first few cleans asking if anything should be done differently.

Your startup checklist

  • Choose your services and service area
  • Check local license and registration requirements
  • Open a separate bank account
  • Get liability insurance quotes
  • Buy starter supplies
  • Set your prices and write a service menu
  • Prepare intake, estimate, checklist, agreement and invoice forms
  • Set up a way to track income and expenses
  • Create a free Google Business Profile
  • Tell 20 people you are open for business
