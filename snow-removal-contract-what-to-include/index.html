Snow Removal Contract: What to Include (With Checklist)

Updated November 25, 2026 · 3 min read

Snow removal is a handshake business for a lot of new operators, right up until the first argument. A customer thinks you should have come at 2 inches, you think the trigger was 3. Their mailbox gets clipped. Someone slips on ice that formed hours after you left. A one-page contract signed before the first storm prevents almost all of it. Here is what to put in it.

1. The parties and the property

Start with the basics: your business name and contact information, the customer's name, phone and email, and the service address. Add a short description of what is being cleared, such as "two-car driveway, front walk to door, three front steps."

Attach a simple site map if the property has anything unusual: where to pile snow, hidden curbs, landscape lights, a gravel section or a drain grate.

2. Trigger depth

The trigger is the amount of snow that starts a visit. Common residential triggers are 1, 2 or 3 inches. Say exactly how it's measured (for example "at the end of the storm, measured on the driveway") and whether you will plow during long storms or wait until the snow stops.

3. Response time

Customers care most about when you'll show up. Give a realistic window, such as "within 8 hours after the snow stops" or "before 7 a.m. for overnight storms." If you prioritize seasonal customers over per-push customers, say so.

4. Services included and extras

List what is included on every visit and what costs extra. For example:

  • Included: driveway, front walk, steps
  • Extra: back patio, mailbox clearing, roof raking, salt or ice melt, hauling snow off site

5. Pricing and payment

Spell out the pricing model the customer chose:

  • Per push: price per visit, plus any heavy storm surcharge (for example, over 6 inches)
  • Seasonal: total price, payment schedule (often two to four installments), and what happens after a cap (for example, 25 pushes or 60 inches)
  • Salting: per application or included

Add your due dates, accepted payment methods and late fee. For seasonal contracts, state that the price is owed whether it snows a lot or a little. That's the trade-off the customer accepted for a fixed price. Our guide on how much to charge for snow removal shows how to set those numbers.

6. Season dates

Name the start and end dates, for example "November 15 through April 15." Snow outside those dates can be billed at your per-push rate.

7. Liability, ice and damage

This section protects you most. Common clauses include:

  • Ice after service: you are not responsible for ice that forms from melting and refreezing after your visit, unless the customer has paid for salting on that visit
  • Marking obstacles: the customer marks curbs, sprinkler heads, landscape lights and edging before the season, and you aren't responsible for damage to unmarked items
  • Existing damage: cracked driveways and loose pavers are noted before the season starts
  • Reporting damage: the customer reports any damage within a set time, such as 48 hours, so you can check it while it's fresh
  • Insurance: you carry general liability insurance (and you should)

Rules about snow and ice liability differ by state and city. Have a local attorney or your insurance agent look over your contract once. It's a small cost compared to a slip-and-fall claim.

8. Cancellation and signatures

Explain how either side can end the agreement, and what happens to prepaid seasonal fees if they do. End with signatures and dates from both of you.

Snow contract checklist

  • Customer and property details, with a site map for tricky properties
  • Trigger depth and how it's measured
  • Response time window
  • Included services and paid extras
  • Pricing model, payment schedule and late fees
  • Season start and end dates
  • Ice, obstacle and damage clauses
  • Cancellation terms and signatures
