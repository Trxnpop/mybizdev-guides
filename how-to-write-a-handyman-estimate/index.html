How to Write a Handyman Estimate That Wins the Job

Updated November 12, 2026 · 3 min read

A clear estimate does two jobs at once. It shows the client you are organized and trustworthy, which wins the work, and it protects you when the scope changes halfway through. A number scribbled on the back of a business card does neither. Here is what a solid handyman estimate includes and how to present it.

Estimate vs quote: know which you are giving

  • An estimate is your best guess. The final price can change if you find hidden problems.
  • A quote (or bid) is a fixed price for a defined scope of work.

Say which one you are giving, right on the page. For repairs with unknowns, an estimate with a stated range protects you. For simple, repeatable jobs, a fixed quote is easier for clients to say yes to.

What to include on every handyman estimate

  1. Your business details. Name, phone, email and license number if your state requires one.
  2. Client details. Name, job address and the best way to reach them.
  3. Estimate number and date. Makes it easy to find later and match to an invoice.
  4. Scope of work. A plain-English description of each task. "Replace kitchen faucet with client-supplied faucet, including new supply lines" beats "Faucet."
  5. Labor. Hours and rate per task, or a flat price per task.
  6. Materials. An itemized list with prices, and whether you or the client supplies them.
  7. Other fees. Disposal, permits, trip charges, after-hours rates.
  8. Total and payment terms. When payment is due and how you accept it. For larger jobs, note any deposit.
  9. Exclusions. What is not included, such as painting after a drywall repair or hauling away an old appliance.
  10. Expiration date. Material prices change. A 14 or 30 day validity window is common.
  11. Approval line. A place for the client to sign or confirm in writing.

A sample estimate layout

Task Labor Materials Total
Replace bathroom faucet (client-supplied faucet) 1.5 hr Supply lines, plumber's tape
Patch two drywall holes in hallway, sand and prime 2 hr (two visits) Patch kit, joint compound, primer
Re-caulk tub surround 1 hr Silicone caulk
Disposal of old fixtures Flat fee

Below the table: subtotal, any discount, tax if your state taxes services or materials, and the total. Then the exclusions, expiration date and signature line.

Handle changes with a work order

Clients almost always ask for "one more thing" once you are there. That is good business, as long as you document it. Write a short work order or change note that lists the new task, the added cost and the client's approval before you start. It avoids arguments at invoice time and builds trust because nothing is a surprise.

Close the job with a sign-off

When the work is done, walk the client through it and have them sign a simple job completion form. It confirms the work met expectations, and it is the perfect moment to ask for a review.

Common estimate mistakes

  • Vague descriptions. "Fix door" can mean anything. Describe the problem and the fix.
  • No exclusions. If you don't paint, haul or move furniture, say so.
  • Forgetting travel and supply runs. These are real costs. Include a trip fee or build them into your rate.
  • No expiration date. A client who accepts six months later will expect the same price.
  • Not getting approval in writing. A text reply saying "Approved" is better than nothing; a signature is better still.
